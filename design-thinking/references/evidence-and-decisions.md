# Judge evidence by the decision it must support

Use this reference when evidence is mixed, indirect, incomplete, or consequential.

## Record one claim per evidence item

Each item should contain:

- a precise claim;
- its evidence label;
- the source and collection date;
- the relevant people, setting, and task;
- known limitations or bias;
- the decision it affects.

Do not place several findings inside one broad claim. Separate items can then support, challenge, or qualify each other.

## Rate strength across five checks

Do not rate evidence by source type alone. Judge it against the claim and decision.

1. **Directness:** Does it show the behavior or outcome, or only describe it?
2. **Relevance:** Does it represent the affected user, context, task, and decision?
3. **Quality:** Was the method capable of answering the question without leading or confounding it?
4. **Recency:** Is the evidence current enough for the behavior, market, policy, or system?
5. **Consistency:** Does independent evidence support it, or do credible contrary cases remain?

Use these ratings:

- **Strong:** Direct and relevant evidence from a sound method, with no unresolved contradiction that would change the decision.
- **Medium:** Useful evidence with a named limitation, proxy, coverage gap, or unresolved contrary case.
- **Weak:** Indirect, stale, poorly matched, biased, anecdotal, or unsupported evidence.

Strength is decision-specific. A short qualitative study can strongly reveal a usability breakdown. It cannot estimate population prevalence.

## Match claims to suitable evidence

| Claim | Better evidence | Common overreach |
|---|---|---|
| People experience a problem | Recent behavior, observation, support or transaction records, repeated first-hand accounts | Treating an internal opinion as a user need |
| The problem is common | Representative quantitative data, repeated operational records, or a defensible sample | Turning interview counts into market percentages |
| People understand a concept | Unprompted explanation, classification, or task choice | Explaining the idea until people agree |
| People can use a response | Realistic task completion, errors, help needed, time, and recovery | Asking whether the interface looks easy |
| People will adopt or pay | Appropriate commitment, repeated use, paid pilot, contract, or real switching behavior | Treating clicks, compliments, or survey intent as adoption |
| The system can work | Focused code test, performance test, integration test, capacity test, and failure analysis | Asking users to judge architecture |
| The operation can work | Manual rehearsal, service trial, staffing and exception data | Testing only the customer-facing screen |
| The model can sustain value | Cost model, retention evidence, channel evidence, and operational data | Repeating a competitor's price without customer context |
| The response is responsible | Affected-user research, accessibility testing, privacy review, misuse cases, and domain review | A generic risk checklist with no affected people |

## Resolve conflicting evidence before averaging it away

When findings conflict:

1. Check whether the sources represent different people, contexts, tasks, or time periods.
2. Check method quality, incentives, recruitment, and missing data.
3. Preserve credible contrary cases in the evidence notes.
4. Decide whether the conflict reveals segments or conditions that need separate responses.
5. Retest only if the conflict could change the decision.

Do not vote across unlike evidence. One observed payment failure can outweigh many positive comments about payment wording.

## Set the evidence threshold before the test

Write a threshold with four parts:

- the observable result;
- the affected group or source;
- the minimum pattern or confidence required;
- the result that would challenge the hypothesis.

Avoid false precision for small qualitative studies. Use behavioral patterns and severe failures, not arbitrary percentages, unless the sample supports statistical inference.

## Make the decision traceable

A recommendation should identify:

- which evidence met or failed the threshold;
- which assumption changed;
- which option now has the best mechanism;
- what risk remains;
- what observation would reopen the decision.

Use `retest` when the method answered the wrong question. Use `revise` when the problem remains credible but the proposed mechanism failed.

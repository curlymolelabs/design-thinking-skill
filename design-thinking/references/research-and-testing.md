# Gather evidence without leading people or overstating results

Use this reference for interviews, observation, surveys, usability studies, demand tests, or field experiments.

## Write the research decision first

State:

- the decision this study will inform;
- the uncertainty and assumption being tested;
- the people and contexts that matter;
- what the method can and cannot establish;
- the evidence threshold and stop rule.

Strong research questions describe behavior or context:

- How do operators notice and recover from failed imports today?
- What evidence do buyers seek before trusting an automatically generated asset?
- Where do people hesitate while comparing two directions?

Weak questions contain the answer:

- Would users like our failure dashboard?
- Is this design intuitive?
- Which of our planned features should we build?

## Recruit for the decision

Define relevant behavior, context, experience, access needs, and exclusion criteria. Recruit actual or likely users for evaluative studies.

Include groups who may face different access, language, device, support, or harm conditions. Do not use coworkers as a silent substitute for target users. If you use them as a pilot, label the result as a pilot.

Record recruitment source and likely bias. Do not claim representation that the sample does not provide.

## Interview recent behavior

Ask about one specific event:

- "Tell me about the last time this happened."
- "What triggered it?"
- "Show me what you did next."
- "Where did you pause, ask for help, or switch tools?"
- "What did that cost in time, money, access, or confidence?"
- "What have you already tried?"

Ask neutral follow-ups. Avoid pitching the response during discovery. Do not ask people to predict behavior far outside their experience.

## Observe before interpreting

Separate notes into:

- action or event;
- exact quote when needed;
- visible hesitation, failure, success, help, or workaround;
- observer interpretation;
- follow-up question.

Capture the environment, collaborators, interruptions, and files, tools, or records that shape behavior. Keep inference out of the observation field.

## Run a task-based usability study

Prepare:

1. a believable goal and starting state;
2. realistic content and required system states;
3. success, failure, and help criteria;
4. moderator silence and rescue rules;
5. follow-up questions after the task;
6. a practice session;
7. consent, recording, privacy, and accessibility controls.

Tell participants that the product is being tested, not them. Record the exact point and level of any help.

Measure behavior that fits the question: completion, critical errors, time, confidence, recovery, comprehension, or repeated use. Do not collapse severe failures into an average score.

## Use surveys for suitable questions

Use surveys for prevalence, segmentation, attitudes, or structured follow-up when a defensible sample is possible. Avoid double questions, leading language, unclear time frames, and answer choices that omit credible responses.

Pilot the survey. Report response rate, sampling limits, missing data, and the exact population represented.

Do not use a survey to discover unknown workflows when observation or interviews would reveal them better.

## Test demand through appropriate commitment

Signals become stronger as they resemble the real adoption path:

1. unprompted return or repeated use;
2. sharing required data or completing setup;
3. joining a scheduled pilot;
4. introducing another decision maker;
5. paying, signing, or switching when appropriate.

Clicks and waitlists can compare messages or channels. They do not prove retention or willingness to pay.

## Synthesize with traceability

Group findings by behavior, context, motivation, barrier, and consequence. Preserve exceptions and credible contrary cases.

Report:

- repeated patterns and differences;
- the evidence behind each finding;
- participant and source limitations;
- assumptions strengthened, weakened, replaced, or still open;
- what the evidence supports;
- what it cannot support;
- the decision effect.

Do not convert a small qualitative sample into population percentages. Do not rewrite the original hypothesis after reviewing results.

## Protect participants and their data

- Obtain informed consent before research, recording, or sharing.
- Collect only data needed for the decision.
- Separate identifying data from ordinary research notes and files.
- Store recordings, transcripts, and sensitive notes under suitable access controls.
- Use synthetic or dummy data when real data cannot be protected.
- Explain incentives, deception risks, withdrawal, and follow-up actions.
- Obtain authorization before contacting people or publishing an experiment.
